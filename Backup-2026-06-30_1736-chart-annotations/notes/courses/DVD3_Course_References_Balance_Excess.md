## Lesson 1: The Diffusion Model — Human Behavior in Markets

### Why Behavior Matters

Before getting into mechanics, Dalton spends time on the *human* side of markets. Why? Because understanding *who is in the market right now* — at what stage of a move — is the key to knowing what will happen next.

The Diffusion Model (popularized by Malcolm Gladwell in *The Tipping Point*) describes how ideas, products, and trends spread through a population:

1. **Innovators** — First in; independent thinkers; take significant early risk; often wrong before being very right
2. **Early Adopters** — Follow the innovators after some evidence; intelligent; get in relatively early
3. **Early Majority** — Follow when the trend becomes "obvious"; large group
4. **Late Majority** — Come in when almost everyone knows; near the end of the move
5. **Laggards** — Last ones in; arrive at the worst possible trade location; mark the exhaustion of the move

### How This Plays Out in Markets

> *"When the tie is finally made — you got the laggard shorts covering, combined with the laggard longs that never got on board. So you got two laggards up there — met by the innovator."*

At a major top or bottom, you have:
- **Laggard longs** (finally bought after watching it go up for months) — they capitulate into the low
- **Laggard shorts** (finally shorted after watching it go up for months) — they pile in at the top

They are met by the innovators — who are doing the opposite. The innovator is selling into the final panic buying. The innovator is buying into the final panic selling.

Paulson's famous mortgage trade is the example. While everyone was flipping homes in Naples, Florida, he was building a short position. He was the innovator. The home flippers were the laggards.

### The Parimutuel Board Analogy

Dalton uses horse racing to illustrate how people get caught in the auction process:

Imagine the betting board stays open until the horse crosses the finish line. As the race unfolds and one horse surges ahead, money floods in on the leader. Rational? No — the odds collapse to essentially nothing. You could bet your life savings and win $2.02 on a $2 bet while risking total loss if the horse stumbles.

> *"People get so caught up in the diffusion model and how they act and their behavior that they forget all about the risk they're taking and what the payoff is going to be. That is not made in jest. That happens in the markets every day."*

### The Research Behind It

Nobel Prize winners Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman devoted careers to studying market irrationality. Their conclusion:

> *"Markets are irrational — but irrational in a predictable manner."*

The diffusion model IS the predictability. The laggard always arrives at the worst time. The innovator always seems "wrong" until suddenly they're very right. Knowing where you are in that cycle changes everything.

## Lesson 2: Reference Points — Your Pre-Market Homework

### Why References Matter

> *"Change is more than likely going to take place relative to a reference. Trading is about change. Without change, there is no opportunity."*

Before every session, you identify the key reference points. These are the price levels where something meaningful happened — where the market previously accepted or rejected a price, where auctions began or ended. When price returns to these levels, you have information. You have context.

### The 11 Reference Points

**Daytime Frame References** (most relevant to scalpers and day traders):

1. **Prior session high and low** — The most basic. Day traders will buy/sell exactly at these mechanical levels.

2. **Overnight high and low** — The electronic market trades 24 hours. The overnight high and low are meaningful. You don't need to track everything overnight, but do track the extremes.

3. **Close / Unchanged** — Prior session's close. Every short-term trader knows where unchanged is. Markets frequently test unchanged before making their directional move.
   > *"There's not a short-term trader in the world that doesn't know what unchanged is."*

4. **Point of Control (POC)** — The fairest price from yesterday. Really good short-term traders know this automatically. The more prominent (longer) the POC, the higher the odds the market returns to it.

5. **Rally high or pullback low** — From the prior session. The afternoon pullback low on a trend day is a specific example of this.

6. **Bracket highs and lows / Trading range extremes** — The top and bottom of a multi-day or multi-week balance area. These are where the market has repeatedly found buyers and sellers.

7. **Spikes** — Late-day price probes (explained in Lesson 4 below).

8. **Current session opening price** — Today's open immediately becomes a daytime frame reference. Every period thereafter is measured relative to it.

9. **Half back** — The midpoint of the day's range as it develops. On days controlled by the day timeframe, half back becomes magnetic.

10. **Double distribution ranges** — The top, bottom, and single prints of each distribution within a double distribution day. Treat each as a separate day's reference.

11. **Longer-term references** — From daily, weekly, and monthly charts. These are the ones most day traders ignore — and get run over by.

### Classifying References by Timeframe

This is a subtle but critical step Dalton recommends: don't just mark the reference — label it.

- **Daytime Frame Reference** = levels only meaningful to scalpers/day traders
- **Short-Term Reference** = 3–5 day range extremes, recent balance areas
- **Intermediate-Term Reference** = weekly chart levels, multi-week bracket extremes
- **Long-Term Reference** = monthly chart levels, multi-month or multi-year highs/lows

> *"The longer the reference, the more likely the change is going to be initiated by a longer timeframe — and the more significant the change will be."*

When you get run over in a market, it's almost always because you missed a longer-term reference. The market reached a level that brought in the intermediate or long-term trader — and you had no idea they were there.

### Preparing References Before the Open

> *"If you aren't prepared, and you're trying to do this once the market opens, the chances of being successful are very small."*

Dalton prints out charts. He marks references over the weekend when there's no noise. He puts them next to his computer. He knows every level before the market opens.

References can be prepared. That's the advantage. Use it.

## Lesson 3: The Spike — A Special Reference

A **spike** is a price probe that happens **late in the day**, either to the upside or downside, where there isn't enough time left to determine if the probe was accepted or rejected.

Think of it as the market "testing" a level after hours. Was that test welcomed or rejected?

**The Three Scenarios for the Following Morning:**

| Opening Location | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Below the spike base (for upside spike) | Spike is rejected; sellers found at that level; excess formed |
| Within the spike | Spike is being accepted; two-sided trade at these levels |
| Above the spike | Market didn't go high enough; buying still hasn't been satisfied; more upside likely |

The **base of an upside spike** becomes support. The **top of a downside spike** becomes resistance. These are clean, visual references you can mark before the open.

## Lesson 4: Anomalies — The Rough Edges

An **anomaly** is a set of TPOs that sticks out from the profile — an irregular edge, a lack of symmetry.

> *"When you have good structure, there'll be some kind of symmetry. When you start to see it lacks symmetry, you start to say, wait a minute, the trading wasn't smooth, maybe it was a little forced."*

The market tends to come back and "smooth out" that anomaly. Very often the anomaly level is where the market stalls or reverses on the following day — because it's the prior session's true POC or value edge.

Dalton spent a full live-trading session pointing at a single anomaly level on a crude oil chart. Most experienced traders in the room were shaking their heads. The next day, the market rallied directly to that exact level and stalled. That's a nuance. Nuances win.

## Lesson 5: The Three Scenarios for Every Balance Area

Before any session where the market is in a multi-day balance, you must be prepared for three things:

**1. The market stays in balance** → Day traders paradise. Trade from the top to the bottom of the range. Regression-to-mean trades. High win rate in the range.

**2. The market looks outside balance and fails** → This is the setup for a destination trade. If it pokes above the balance and fails, the destination is the opposite end of the balance.

**3. The market breaks out of balance and goes** → Go with it. Do not fade. Increasing volume confirms.

Coming in with all three scenarios pre-planned means you react to what actually happens — not to what surprises you.

> *"You are going to be very much harder to compete against when you come in prepared with your references and scenarios compared with someone just winging it."*

## Lesson 6: EXCESS — The End of One Auction, the Start of Another

### What Excess Is

Excess is the **decisive rejection** of a price level — a tail, a gap, a spike, or single prints that form when the market moved too far in one direction and got pushed back hard.

> *"Excess marks the end of one auction and the beginning of another auction."*

It happens in every timeframe:
- A 5-minute buying tail = daytime frame excess
- A daily gap = short-term excess
- A monthly spike high = long-term excess

The concept is identical regardless of timeframe. The bigger the timeframe of the excess, the more significant the reversal.

### Excess Is the Moment of Greatest Risk AND Greatest Reward

> *"Excess is the moment of greatest risk. It is also the moment of greatest reward if you are positioned correctly."*

If you're **long** when excess forms on the upside — you just witnessed the end of the buying auction. That's the greatest risk: you're holding inventory at the worst possible location.

If you're **short** when excess forms on the upside — you're in the best possible trade. You just caught the turn.

The challenge is execution, not identification:

> *"When the auction goes up and the excess happens on the high, the excess is going to occur in the face of the most recent market activity. So what your eyes just saw was a market making a new high. And it falls away from it. And I'm telling you what your position should be — short. How hard is that trade to do?"*

This is why most traders can't execute on excess. Everything they just watched was going up. Their instinct says buy, not sell. The understanding of excess says the opposite.

### Excess and the Auction Process

Return to the art auction analogy: the auctioneer squeezed the last bid out at $150,000. If he now says "I have one more of the same thing," it shouldn't go for more than $125,000 — the auction is complete. The price should fall away quickly.

That's excess. The completed auction. The price that shouldn't be paid again anytime soon.

### Excess Starts the Trade Location Process Over

Once excess forms, you have a new reference: the extreme of the prior auction. That becomes your anchor for the new auction. Trade location now starts fresh from that new reference.

## Lesson 7: BALANCE — Paradise for Day Traders

### What Balance Is

The market is in balance approximately **85% of trading days** — meaning there's meaningful overlap between adjacent sessions. Price hasn't moved decisively in any direction. Both sides are comfortable in the current range.

Short-term balance (a few days to a few weeks) is the day trader's best friend:

> *"Short-term balance is absolute paradise for short-term traders. You're trading from the top to the bottom of the balance. It's paradise there."*

The trade is simple: buy the low end of the balance, sell the high end. Regression to mean. High probability, well-defined risk.

### The Trap: Doing What Works Until It Doesn't

> *"How many times do you hear me say — traders do what works until it doesn't work anymore."*

The danger of balance paradise: when the market finally breaks out, traders fade the breakout. They've been so successful selling the top of the range that they can't help themselves — they sell the breakout too.

> *"On day five, you break out from the top of the five-day balance. Most traders are going to do what? They're going to fade the breakout. And they're absolutely as wrong as wrong can be."*

The solution: know that balance doesn't last forever. Be prepared for the breakout. When volume confirms and the break is clean — go with it. Don't sell it.

### Where Balance Occurs = Age of the Trend

This is a critical insight about longer timeframes:

- **Balance near the bottom of a long decline** → Young trend to the upside may be beginning
- **Balance in the middle of a move** → Consolidation; trend likely continues
- **Balance near the top of a long rally** → Trend is aging; innovator sellers may be positioning

The location of balance within the broader structure tells you what chapter of the story you're in.

### Monthly Balance Example

Dalton walks through a monthly chart example: a market that has been balancing for months finally forms excess at one extreme. The market then trades all the way to the opposite extreme of the balance — a destination trade spanning months.

The same logic that works on a 5-minute chart works on a monthly chart. The concepts are fractal.

## Lesson 8: The Destination Trade

When a market **looks above a reference and fails** (creates excess at a high), the most likely next destination is the **opposite end of the balance or range**.

> *"Once there is excess, we should begin to think destination. If I fail at this end of balance, the destination trade is down here to the opposite end of balance."*

This doesn't mean it has to get there. But it gives you a target — something to measure the auction against as it unfolds. Is it moving toward the destination? Is it stalling? Is it reversing?

Practical application:
- Market breaks above 3-day balance and fails → destination is the low of that 3-day range
- Market breaks above a weekly bracket high and fails → destination is the weekly bracket low
- Market gaps below a prior low (excess) → destination is the high of the prior balance

The destination trade gives you a context for how far the new auction can run.

## Lesson 9: Imagination — The Required Mental Skill

Dalton makes a point that surprises people: one of the most important trading skills is **imagination**.

> *"Many traders can't expand their imagination to move beyond the intuitive and misleading concept that there is more risk to paying 12 than there is paying 10 for something."*

When a market breaks to new highs, the imagination-deficient trader says: "Too expensive, I'm not buying that." The trader with imagination says: "Excess was cleared, balance was broken, new auction has begun — where is this going?"

> *"My biggest mistakes I ever make is I get so focused on a market because it's lulled me to sleep. It's been in a kind of tight trading range. You just can't imagine that this market could break out and just go like crazy. And it happens all the time."*

Excess + breakout from balance requires you to picture a move that is **bigger than what you've recently been seeing**. That takes imagination. Develop it deliberately.

## Lesson 10: Being a Professional Trader — Adapting to Market Conditions

Dalton pushes back hard against the popular advice to "find what you do best and specialize in it."

> *"Here's something that I disagree with almost everybody that teaches about how to work and trade with markets. People will tell you: 'Figure out what it is you do best, then concentrate on that.' I don't believe that. If you're going to be a professional trader, you have got to learn to be able to adjust to what the market is doing."*

Markets shift between regimes:
- **Balance regime** → regression to mean trades; fade the extremes
- **Trend/breakout regime** → go with the break; do not fade

Professional traders adapt. Amateur traders specialize in one style and get destroyed when the market regime changes.

> *"Market breaks out of the trading range, then you've got to be a more aggressive trend-type trader and go with the breakout. You have to be able to trade what is going on in the market."*

2. A long-term trend has been running for 6 months. The market then balances at the top for 3 weeks. Using the Diffusion Model, who is buying right now? Who is likely beginning to sell?

3. You see a spike to the upside late on Friday. Monday morning the market opens *within* the spike. What does this tell you? What is your bias for Monday's session?

4. A market breaks to new 52-week highs. Your colleague says "I'm not buying that — it's too expensive." Using Dalton's framework, what is the counterargument?

5. The market looks above a 5-day balance high and immediately reverses back into the balance. What trade is now set up? What is the destination?
