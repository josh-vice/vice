## Lesson 1: The Primary Day — Your Benchmark

Before you can understand what a market is doing, you need a baseline to measure it against. Dalton uses the **Primary Day** as that benchmark — the market's equivalent of the bell-shaped curve.

A Primary Day is a relatively normal, rotational day. Both sides participate. The market auctions higher to find sellers, auctions lower to find buyers, and ends up forming a reasonably balanced bell curve shape. The POC sits near the center of the range.

> *"My bell-shaped curve becomes the primary day. If you were looking at data and you saw quite a skew to a bell-shaped curve, you'd know something was wrong."*

When the day deviates significantly from the primary day structure — that deviation carries information. How much skew? Which direction? That's where you start to read what the market is communicating.

A **Variation of a Primary Day** is just a primary day with more directional aggressiveness — slightly more skewed, slightly more conviction from one side. Not enough to be a trend day, but more than a normal rotational session.

## Lesson 2: The 5 Day Types

### Day Type 1: Primary Day
- Normal bell curve shape
- Both sides participate
- POC near center of range
- Strategy: rotate; trade both sides; buy value area low, sell value area high

### Day Type 2: Variation of Primary
- Slightly more directional
- Skew toward one end
- Still rotational but with a lean
- Strategy: bias toward the direction of skew but still trade rotational moves

### Day Type 3: Normal Trend Day
- Market moves in one direction for most of the session
- Very narrow profile — almost a straight line vertically
- POC at or near one extreme
- Strategy: **do not fade it**

> *"You do not fade a trend day. It is amazing how many traders, when I look over their shoulder, cannot resist fading the trend day."*

On a trend day, your job is simple: get on as early as possible and stay on. If you need to do short-term trades, **take profits on rallies and buy the pullback** — do not short rallies and cover on breaks. You're already long. Work with the trend.

> *"If you, in fact, are going to short a trend day, you have to be right immediately."*

### Day Type 4: Double Distribution Trend Day
- Two separate value areas form — one early, one after a migration
- A gap or single prints separate the two distributions
- The breakout midday creates the second distribution

This is subtler than the normal trend day. Key insight: **the double distribution trend day has greater odds of retracing** than the normal trend day. Why? Because the normal trend day represents pure conviction all day. The double distribution represents two balancing acts — there's more ambiguity in the structure.

If you miss the first distribution, trade the second distribution as if it's an independent day. The bottom of the second distribution becomes critical support — if price reenters it, that's bearish for the short term.

### Day Type 5: Balanced Day
- Market auctions both ways, stays within a tight range
- POC near center
- Market closes near where it opened

> *"When a market is balanced, the message that a balanced market is delivering is: the market wants more information before it makes its next directional auction."*

Markets speak. The balanced day says: *I don't know yet.* Respect it. Don't force trades. Let the market come to you.

## Lesson 3: What Makes Market Profile Unique

Dalton spends time on this because too many people approach the profile mechanically. They read forums, find rules like "buy the value area low, sell the value area high," and try to make it a system.

That misses the entire point.

What makes the profile unique is **synthesis combined with analysis**:

- **Synthesis** = combining all the separate elements into a whole picture (like the Napoleon graphic)
- **Analysis** = breaking it apart to study individual components (day type, tails, POC, volume)

> *"The profile does not tell you to buy. It does not tell you to sell. It doesn't do any of that. It is very quiet."*

The profile organizes the data generated by the two-way auction. Your job is to extract the information from that data. That requires judgment, context, and experience — not mechanical rules.

## Lesson 4: Price vs. Value — The Most Important Distinction

### Price Is an Advertisement

This is the conceptual shift that separates traders who understand markets from those who don't:

> *"Price advertises opportunity. Time regulates opportunity. Volume measures the success or failure of the advertised opportunity."*

Price is nothing more than the **advertised price**. It tells you where the market is right now. It doesn't tell you if that price is fair, accepted, or sustainable.

When you combine time (how long the market spent at a price) with volume (how much business was done there), you get **value**. Value is the composite. Value is what actually matters.

> *"The most difficult thing you will deal with — if you get interested in what we're talking about — is getting away from price. It is addictive. Our minds like price because it does linear."*

### When Did You Know You Internalized It?

Dalton describes his personal turning point:

> *"When somebody would ask me, 'What did the market do today?' — the first thing coming out of my head would be, 'Value was higher today.' And of course, when you're talking to someone who has no idea what you're talking about, that is not an answer that gets a lot of appreciation. But I knew I was starting to take this information to the next level."*

When your brain automatically thinks in terms of value — not price — you've made the transition.

### The Key Question: Will Price Return to Value, or Will Value Follow Price?

Whenever price and value disagree, you face this fundamental question:

- **Will price snap back to value?** (Regression to the mean — the more common scenario, ~85% of days)
- **Will value follow price?** (New trend beginning — less common but the bigger opportunity)

Understanding which scenario is playing out is the core analytic challenge.

If price moves away from value on **low volume** → odds favor regression to mean  
If price moves away from value on **high volume** → odds favor value following price (trend)

## Lesson 5: Value Area Relationships

Every day, you have a new value area relative to yesterday's. The relationship between them is meaningful:

| Scenario | What It Means |
|---|---|
| Higher value, higher price | Bullish — both confirm |
| Lower value, lower price | Bearish — both confirm |
| Higher value, lower price | Value driving; price likely to follow up |
| Lower value, higher price | Price leading; danger — may snap back to value |
| Overlapping value areas | Balance; neither side in control |

The **overlapping value** scenario is very common (~85% of days). The market hasn't broken anywhere meaningful — it's balanced. Most rotational trades occur within these overlapping value areas.

When value clearly shifts higher or lower day over day — that's a **value area trend**. The longer it persists, the more meaningful the directional move.

## Lesson 6: The 4 Opening Types

This is one of the most actionable sections of Dalton's entire course. The opening tells you how much conviction is behind the day's initial move — and that determines your urgency to act.

### Opening Type 1: Open Drive (Highest Confidence)

The market opens and **immediately drives** in one direction with no hesitation. The opening price will be at or very near the low (for an up day) or high (for a down day) of the entire session.

> *"It is the highest confidence opening you will see. The higher the confidence of the opening, the less the odds that you will come back through that opening price."*

**Strategy:** Act immediately. Don't wait. Don't try to get a better price.

> *"In a high-confidence open, you have to go to the market. Don't be a perfectionist. Don't nitpick it. Get on the market."*

### Opening Type 2: Open Test Drive (Second Highest Confidence)

The market opens, **tests in the opposite direction first**, then drives the intended direction. Very often the "test" goes to a meaningful reference point — prior day's POC, overnight low, monthly high, etc.

When it finds buyers/sellers at that reference and reverses, the drive is underway. Second highest conviction because it tested and confirmed.

**Strategy:** Watch for the test to a reference and the rejection. Enter when the reversal is confirmed.

### Opening Type 3: Open Rejection Reversal (Medium Confidence)

The opening moves in one direction for a longer period — enough to feel like a trend is forming — then gets **rejected aggressively** and reverses. It had to go further to find the opposing side, but it found them.

This is less reliable than the first two because you had to wait longer. The reversal can also look like a potential trend day early on, creating confusion.

**Strategy:** Wait for the rejection to be confirmed by aggressive price movement in the opposite direction before acting.

### Opening Type 4: Open Auction (Low Confidence)

The market opens and goes back and forth through the opening price multiple times. No conviction from either side. The market is figuring itself out.

> *"If it goes back and forth through the opening price multiple times, what do you think the level of conviction is? Pretty low. Do you need to trade early or late? Trade late. Absolutely. Sit back. Let the market come to you."*

**Strategy:** Be patient. Do not trade early. Let the market develop. In an open auction, early trading is guessing — not trading.

## Lesson 7: Opening Location — The Four Scenarios

The *type* of opening tells you conviction. The *location* of the opening tells you opportunity size.

### Scenario 1: Open Within Yesterday's Value
Smallest opportunity. The market woke up exactly where it fell asleep. Both sides are comfortable here. There's no urgency to buy or sell.

### Scenario 2: Open Within Yesterday's Range but Outside Value
Moderate opportunity. Price has moved from value but is still within the prior range. The question becomes: will value follow price, or will price return to value?

### Scenario 3: Open Outside Yesterday's Range (Within Value Area)
Larger opportunity. The market has already moved beyond the prior session's extremes. Something is different today.

### Scenario 4: Open Outside Yesterday's Range AND Outside Value
**Maximum opportunity.** The market is out of balance on both dimensions. Either it will continue aggressively (value is being repriced) or it will snap back violently. Either way — big move.

> *"The biggest opportunities occur when we are out of balance."*

## Lesson 8: The 5 Timeframes — Who Is in Control?

Markets are not monolithic. At any given moment, multiple timeframes are participating simultaneously. Understanding **who is dominating** determines your strategy.

### The 5 Timeframes

1. **Scalper** — Seconds to minutes; high frequency; trades the spread
2. **Day Timeframe** — Within the session; buys yesterday's high, sells yesterday's low
3. **Short-Term** — 3–5 days; swing within a developing trend
4. **Intermediate-Term** — Weeks to months; hedge funds, CTA's; moves markets meaningfully
5. **Long-Term** — Months to years; Fidelity, Vanguard, JP Morgan; creates the biggest moves

### How to Tell Who's In Control

> *"A day timeframe trader — they will buy yesterday's high, they will sell yesterday's low. The long timeframe? They could care less."*

If the market opens at the edge of the prior range and barely goes one tick beyond then comes back — that's day timeframe control. Small, mechanical, reference-based.

If the market opens and moves **quickly and aggressively** through yesterday's range — that's at minimum short-term timeframe activity. Longer timeframes don't care about yesterday's tick-precise levels.

> *"Timeframe behavior reveals itself as the sessions unfold. This enables the trader to discern who is dominating the market — and subsequently, what strategy and tactics to utilize."*

### The Big Money Reality

Dalton ran an institutional trading desk. He explains how large institutions actually trade:

> *"If I go and buy all of what I'm supposed to buy at one time, the chances that I'm going to give a customer the best fill of the day are pretty slim. I would prefer to give them an average fill."*

Big money buys in waves. Sells in waves. The profile captures the net effect of those waves — subtle, sustained migration of the POC in one direction while price goes the other way. That's the 45-degree line again.

### Cognitive Dissonance

> *"The market structure may say the market's strong, but they've heard the commentator say the number's negative — as if the commentator really knew. Cognitive dissonance is trying to hold two thoughts."*

If market structure is strong and a headline is negative, you may get a knee-jerk sell-off — which resolves back upward because the structure was right and the headline was noise. Traders who can't separate structure from headline will trade the wrong way.

## Lesson 9: The Afternoon Pullback Low

This is one of the "nuances" Dalton mentions — something almost no one knows about but that changes how you read end-of-day data.

On a trend day (upward), the market often makes a brief **inventory adjustment late in the afternoon**. This looks like a small pullback. Many traders misread it as a reversal and go short.

> *"The afternoon pullback low. If the market trends up from the C period, balances late in the day — that K period pullback low is a significant short-term reference point."*

If the following day doesn't trade **below that afternoon pullback low**, nothing has changed. The trend is intact.

> *"If you don't understand structure, you don't understand how to look at a market — you may see price going down toward that level. And if you're just trading price, you want to get short. You can't wait to get short. And all of a sudden, the market turns around and goes back up."*

## Lesson 10: The Live Market — Applying It in Real Time

Dalton demonstrates with live bond and crude oil charts during the session. Key observations:

**Bonds that day:** Open auction — went back and forth through the opening 8 periods with no confidence. The right call: sit on your hands. Wait.

He specifically notes unchanged (prior close) as a critical short-term reference: when the market can't reach unchanged, watch where it goes next. Going up, failing at unchanged, then returning to the open — that's a complete short-term daytime frame story.

**Crude that day:** Opened outside value but within the prior range. Auctioned lower and **didn't take the prior day's low** — that was a buy signal. The market then went back to test the anomaly (rough edge) from the prior session — the exact fairest price from yesterday.

> *"Most people wouldn't do that. Most people would have no idea about that silly little price or that silly little anomaly. That was the fairest price at which business was being conducted yesterday."*

## Study Questions

1. You open a chart and the market has driven hard in one direction from the open with no pullback through the opening price. What opening type is this? What is your urgency to trade?

2. Yesterday's value area was 4400–4420. Today the market opens at 4380 (below yesterday's range and value). What is the size of the opportunity? What are the two scenarios you must be prepared for?

3. A market has been trending up for 3 days. Today it opens and immediately pulls back through yesterday's high — but only by 2 ticks — then drives back up. Who is in control of this day? Why?

4. The market shows "higher price, lower value" at the end of the day. As a trader coming in tomorrow, what is the primary question you must answer?

5. At 3:45 PM on a trend day, the S&P pulls back 8 points. Everyone around you is shorting. What do you know about this pullback that they don't?
