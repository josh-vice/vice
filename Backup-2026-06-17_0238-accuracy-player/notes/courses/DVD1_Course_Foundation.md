## Lesson 1: What Is a Market?

### The Core Truth

At its foundation, the market is **a simple two-way auction process**. The motives and incentives of buyers and sellers are virtually infinite — but the mechanism is always the same.

Dalton starts every program with this because people wildly overcomplicate markets. They think about earnings, Fed policy, geopolitics. All of that feeds *into* the auction. But the auction itself is simple:

- The market auctions **higher** when it is searching for **sellers**
- The market auctions **lower** when it is searching for **buyers**
- The auction continues until both sides find each other and two-sided trade can occur

### The Art Auction Example

Dalton uses a live auction to make this visceral. If an auctioneer opens at $100,000 and no one bids, what does he do? He **lowers the price** until buyers show up. Once bidding starts, it may rocket back through $100,000 and beyond — because now higher prices are **attracting more activity**, not cutting it off.

Key insight from that example:

> *"When you hear the auctioneer say 'new bidder' — higher prices are bringing in more activity, not cutting off the activity. Guess what? It doesn't make any difference what you think that value is. That auction is not completed."*

This plays out in markets every day. Price goes down and you won't buy it. Price starts back up and suddenly you're paying 9 for something you could have had at 7. You got **caught in the auction process**.

### One-Way vs. Two-Way Auctions

Traditional auctions (art, livestock) go in **one direction only** — up. But financial markets run a **continuous two-way auction**: constantly searching up for sellers, then down for buyers, in an endless cycle.

The Market Profile graphic exists to **record the net effect of that two-way auction process.**

## Lesson 2: Why Organize the Data?

### The Napoleon Graphic

Dalton references what he calls the most famous graphic in the world — the visualization of Napoleon's army going into Russia. From a single image, you can see:
- How large the army was at the start
- How it shrank along the way
- How temperature dropped
- How food supplies dwindled

**One graphic. Enormous complexity. Instantly understandable.**

That's what the Market Profile graphic does for a trading session.

### The Distribution Curve

Scientists have used distribution curves for decades to organize data so it can be studied. The Market Profile does the same thing for market data.

The breakthrough came from a man named Pete Steidlmayer, who studied statistics at Stanford. His key discovery:

> *"You can make time the constant. The time between 11 and 11:30 is the same as the time between 12 and 12:30. One day is as long as the next day."*

**Time (constant) vs. Price (variable)** — plot one against the other and you have the makings of a distribution curve that organizes market data in real time.

Dalton knew immediately when Steidlmayer pitched this idea. He wrote a $10,000 check on the spot to sponsor Steidlmayer's first book, *Markets and Market Logic*. That's how certain he was about the value of this discovery.

### What the Distribution Reveals

The barbecue analogy: If you have 30 people at a party and someone asks you the average height, you can't answer just by looking around. But if you organize those same 30 people under a distribution curve — you can see it immediately.

Now take two "photos" at different times and overlay them. You see **shifts in the underlying data** that you'd never catch just by observing. That's market-generated information: subtle, real, and unbiased.

## Lesson 3: Building the Profile — TPOs Explained

### What a TPO Is

Each letter on the profile is called a **Time Price Opportunity (TPO)**. It is nothing more than a record that the market **traded at a given price during a given 30-minute period.**

- Period A = first 30 minutes of the session
- Period B = second 30 minutes
- Period C = third 30 minutes
- ...and so on

If the market traded at a price in three separate 30-minute periods, you'll see three letters at that price. If it traded there for only one period, you'll see one letter.

The more letters at a price, the more **time** the market spent there. Time = acceptance. Acceptance = value.

### What Emerges

As TPOs accumulate throughout the day, you get a two-dimensional picture of the session. You can now see:

- **Where did the market spend the most time?** (Center of the distribution = fairest price)
- **Where did it spend the least time?** (Edges = rejected prices)
- **What direction did it extend?** (Range extension reveals who's driving)

Compare that to a bar chart, which gives you only: open, high, low, close. Four data points. One dimension. Zero context.

Dalton's point on more information:

> *"On its surface, while it intuitively may sound good that more information is better — it is not necessarily true. You have to use that information very intelligently."*

## Lesson 4: Initial Balance and Range Extension

### What Is Initial Balance?

The **Initial Balance (IB)** is roughly the first hour of trading — the first two 30-minute periods (A and B). It's not a strict rule; it's a concept.

Think of it this way: when the market opens, both sides test. Buyers come in, sellers push back, and a rough range gets established early. That's your initial balance.

> *"What you want to see is, as the day goes on, are you getting outside of that range?"*

### Range Extension

If the market breaks below the IB low in the C period, it found buyers down there — it extended the range. If it breaks above the IB high, sellers pulled it back. Range extension tells you:

1. **Which direction the market wants to go**
2. **Who is driving — buyers or sellers**
3. **Whether a trend day may be developing**

A market that stays within its initial balance all day is a **balanced day** (covered in DVD 2). A market that extends significantly in one direction is showing **directional conviction**.

## Lesson 5: Buying Tails and Selling Tails

### What a Tail Tells You

A **buying tail** is a cluster of single TPOs at the low end of the profile where the market traded very briefly before shooting back up. It means:

- Buyers were **aggressive** at that level
- The market found value quickly and moved away
- That price level is a meaningful reference

The longer the tail, the more aggressive the buyers were. A 2-tick tail is a whisper. A 10-tick tail is a shout.

A **selling tail** is the mirror image — aggressive sellers at the top who drove price away quickly.

### The Warning: One Data Point

Here's where Dalton issues an important caution. When people first learn about buying tails, they immediately want to go find stocks or futures with buying tails and trade them.

> *"This is one data point. It is one piece of information. It is not in context."*

A buying tail without context is just a shape. What matters is: **where is it relative to value? What timeframe is in control? What was the prior session doing?**

### What No Tail Means

Equally important — the absence of a tail is also information. A market with no buying tail at the low means the auction at that level is **incomplete**. The market went down there, didn't find strong buyers, and bounced — but it hasn't finished its business down there.

> *"When you don't have the buying or selling tails, what we're saying is the puzzle at this point is not yet completed."*

Incomplete auctions get revisited. That market is likely to come back and test that low again.

## Lesson 6: The Point of Control (POC)

### Definition

The **Point of Control** is the longest line of TPOs in the daily profile — the price where the market spent the most time.

Technical definition: longest line closest to the center of the range.

But Dalton's preferred definition is more meaningful:

> *"It is the fairest price at which business is being conducted."*

That's not just a label. It's a philosophy. The POC is where the most two-sided trade took place — where buyers and sellers most agreed on value for that session.

### Why the POC Matters

**It acts like a magnet.** The market is always aware of the fairest price. It is very hard to move away from it and stay away. If price moves above the POC on low volume, the odds of it snapping back are high. If it moves away on strong volume, the odds lessen.

**It is dynamic.** The POC is not fixed at the open. It migrates throughout the day as new TPOs form. Watching which direction it migrates tells you a great deal about who is in control.

**It regresses to the mean.** Think like a business owner: if you raise your price and volume drops off, you know immediately the market won't support that price. The same dynamic exists here.

### Volume Away from the POC

- Price moves **above** the POC on **low volume** → high probability of snapping back (regression to mean)
- Price moves **above** the POC on **high volume** → lower probability of snap-back; trend may be developing
- Price moves **below** the POC on **low volume** → same — likely to return
- Price moves **below** the POC on **high volume** → selling is real; possible structural change

## Lesson 7: POC Migration and the 45-Degree Line

### What Migration Tells You

As the day goes on, watch where the POC moves:

- **POC migrating higher** → buyers are in control; each new TPO is building a higher fairest price
- **POC migrating lower** → sellers are distributing; they're likely selling every rally subtly
- **POC not migrating** while price moves → critical signal

### "Short in the Hole" — The 45-Degree Line

This is one of Dalton's most powerful real-time signals. On April 21, 2010, he sent an email to the hedge funds he advised at 2:51 PM that said:

> *"Look at the POC, not migrating lower. Sellers are getting caught short in the hole. 45-degree line. No outside event and we could be setting up for a rally."*

Here's what was happening: price was going **lower**, but the POC was **not moving lower with it**. This means traders were getting short at bad prices — they were "short in the hole."

When the POC doesn't migrate with price, it means:
1. The fairest price is still up at the old level
2. Sellers are chasing price down, building short positions at poor locations
3. The market is likely to snap back to where value is (the POC)

This is a setup. When those shorts realize they're trapped, covering creates a fast rally.

### Why Big Money Hides Its Intentions

> *"If you're Fidelity or Magellan or Invesco, you don't want to advertise what you're doing. It's not uncommon for these people to spread orders out or do everything they can to disguise them."*

The migrating POC is one way big money reveals itself despite trying to hide. They can't disguise the fact that time spent at the fairest price is moving in one direction.

## Lesson 8: Market Structure

### Structure vs. a Bar Chart

A bar chart gives you a line. A profile gives you **structure** — a two-dimensional shape that reveals whether the market's foundations are strong or weak.

Strong structure: clean profile, good tails on both ends, POC near center, well-defined value area. The market has done its job. Two-sided trade took place in an organized way.

Weak structure: poor highs or lows (no tails), anomalies (jagged edges), POC near an extreme, thin profile. The market's business is unfinished.

### Why Structure Matters

> *"If the underlying structure of that market had been strong, I suspect we would not have had that kind of break."*

Dalton referenced the famous 1,000-point Dow drop. He had been writing about weak structure for months before it happened. He didn't predict the magnitude — that was a black swan event — but he understood that weak structure **increases the probability** of something going wrong.

Think of it like a house with bad shingles. The house might stand for years. But in a big storm, weak structure fails in ways that strong structure doesn't.

### Structure Repair

Poor highs and lows are like open wounds. The market will come back to repair them — "smooth out the profile." When a poor low is revisited and finally gets a tail, the structure has been repaired. The auction is now complete.

## Lesson 9: Market-Generated Information vs. Opinion

### The Most Important Distinction

> *"It's not somebody's opinion. It's not somebody on the news telling you anything. This is real. This happens with real people, with real money. It's not somebody's view on the tube or a talking head. This is supported by real money, real activity."*

Every TPO on the profile represents an actual trade. Actual money. Someone actually bought or sold at that price in that period. The profile is a record of reality — not analysis of what someone *thinks* reality is.

News, analysts, headlines — all of that is opinion. The profile is **fact**.

### Context Is Everything

> *"No real learning starts until you can put that information within some contextual view."*

Every piece of information on the profile — tails, POC, value area, structure — only means something **in context**:
- Context of where we opened relative to yesterday's range and value
- Context of what timeframe is in control
- Context of the larger trend
- Context of volume

A buying tail in isolation is nothing. A buying tail at a 6-month balance low after an excess move on high volume in a trending market — that's something.

## Dalton's Core Message from DVD 1
