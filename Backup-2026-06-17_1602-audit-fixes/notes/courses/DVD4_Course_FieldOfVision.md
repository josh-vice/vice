## Lesson 1: The Field of Vision — What It Means

### The Name

The course title came from a conversation about Megan Rapinoe playing field hockey. A teammate described how Megan never made an angle shot at the goal — she always shot straight and hard. No wasted motion. No erratic choices.

The teammate said: *"She had a great field of vision."*

Dalton heard that and knew immediately — that's what great trading looks like. You're not staring at the screen hunched over, locked onto price. You're sitting back, taking in the whole landscape, seeing the full field.

> *"You're taking in to do these really good trades — you have to have the field of vision in mind. You can't make erratic shots. One of the things that goes on — you get this great opportunity coming up and you're making some little insignificant trade. It's the wrong direction. And what's the problem with a bad trade? It keeps you out of a good trade."*

A bad trade doesn't just cost you money. It costs you position — it puts you on the wrong side when the real opportunity arrives. The field of vision means: see the whole board before you act.

### The Three Pillars (Again)

Everything in the course traces back to this framework:

> *"Price advertises opportunity. Time regulates the opportunity. Volume measures the success or failure of the advertised opportunity."*

When you combine all three into value — **that** is what you trade. Not price. Value.

## Lesson 2: Volume — The Most Misused Tool in Trading

### Why "Volume Doesn't Matter" Is Wrong

Most academic research concludes that volume doesn't matter. Dalton dismantles this:

> *"What that really means is they haven't figured out how to use volume yet. When people do research, they buy streams of data from universities — only price data. There's no context. They don't know if the price was made on old business or new business. They treat all prices as equal. Anybody in any kind of business intuitively knows that all prices are not equal."*

If you raise your product's price and volume drops — you know that's not sustainable. If you raise the price and volume increases or holds steady — you've got something. The same logic applies to markets.

### Rule 1: Volume Must Be Used Contextually

Volume does not mean the same thing in all situations. There are two completely different volume contexts:

**Volume in a Balancing Area:**
> *"Volume in a balance area is not nearly as important. The market's just going back and forth on a balancing area. Volume isn't insignificant — but it's relatively unimportant."*

When the market is in balance, it's found a place where two-sided trade naturally occurs. Both sides are happy-ish. Volume just measures the pace of that rotation. Nothing more.

**Volume in a Breakout:**
> *"Where volume is really important is when I break out. If I break out of this four-day balance to the upside — in order to get away from the fairest price to do business, I'm going to need volume. If I don't have volume, the fairest price is going to act very much like a magnet and pull price back."*

When the market breaks out of balance, volume is the tell. Strong volume = new participants entering, the move is real. Weak volume = old participants repositioning, the move may fail.

### Rule 2: Old Business vs. New Business

This distinction is fundamental:

- **Old business** = short covering (people buying to close existing shorts) or long liquidation (people selling to close existing longs)
- **New business** = fresh money entering the market in the direction of the move

Old business weakens a market even while it pushes price in a direction. Short covering can take a market up — but when the shorts are done covering, there's no fuel left.

New business sustains a move. Innovators entering. Real directional conviction.

> *"Old business — short covering. Does short covering weaken or strengthen a market? It weakens a market because it takes buying power out of it."*

On the profile, old business driving a rally produces a "P" formation (short covering — round top, tail low). New business driving a rally produces elongation with the POC migrating higher.

**Monitoring for Continuation:**
- **Elongation** (profile stretching in the direction of the move) = new business, move is healthy
- **No elongation** = old business only; move may stall
- **POC migrating in direction of price** = healthy
- **POC not migrating with price** = weakness; the 45-degree signal again

### Rule 3: Attempted Direction Determines Volume Relevance

> *"Volume is not relevant if you don't know what the market was trying to do."*

If the market opens in the middle and goes up 5, then down 5, then up 5 — there's no clear attempted direction. Volume is meaningless in that context.

But if the market opens on the low and drives higher — attempted direction is clear: up. Now volume becomes highly relevant. Strong volume on the upward drive = healthy. Diminishing volume on the upward drive = concern.

> *"Without knowing attempted direction, volume will terribly, terribly mislead you."*

### Rule 4: Volume Can Dry Up at Major References — and That's OK

Counter-intuitive but important: when a market approaches a major reference (a multi-month high, a major bracket extreme), volume may dry up — even if the market is about to break through.

Why? Because experienced traders exit on this side of the reference rather than risk being wrong on the other side. They'll re-enter once the reference is cleared. So the volume dip at the reference isn't necessarily bearish — it may just be professional traders lightening up before the breakout is confirmed.

> *"It would be very logical that I come out at this side [before the reference], even though this one went through. Take it off at a major reference. Put it back on if it breaks out on the other side."*

### Rule 5: Use the Best Volume Source

- **Trading currencies or bond futures**: futures volume is your only option
- **Trading S&Ps or equities**: use NYSE composite volume — it's a better, broader, more reliable source than S&P futures volume alone

Dalton tracks volume by 30-minute period, accumulating throughout the day. He knows what a "normal" volume day looks like and can quickly sense when volume is running heavy or light relative to the time of day.

## Lesson 3: The Institutional Mindset — Why Markets Go to Extremes

### Nobody Wants to Be First

This section explains a phenomenon every trader has puzzled over: why do markets keep going when everyone knows they're overextended?

> *"Let's say you are the senior portfolio manager for a very large fund. You are the first one out of the market — and you're absolutely right. But so quickly you get lost within the crowd. You don't get any special credit for being the first one. Now, let's say you're the first one to sell and nobody else sells. Now you're out there on a limb by yourself. And the example: 'Why in the world would you sell that? Nobody else was selling.' If you're wrong — your job may be at stake."*

This is the institutional trap. Being right early and alone is **more dangerous for a portfolio manager's career** than being wrong with everyone else. So they all stay in too long. They all lean against the door frame.

> *"As the CEO of Citibank was asked why they didn't get out of some of this toxic stuff — his answer was: 'Well, everybody was still dancing.' They claim they saw the risk, but they didn't get out because everybody was still dancing."*

That's how markets go to extremes. And that's where excess forms — when the last dancer finally stops.

## Lesson 4: Preparation — The Foundation of Everything

> *"Virtually all performance literature recognizes the connection between performance and preparation. Without preparation, we are unlikely to overcome the emotions that really cost us dearly in the market."*

Preparation is not optional. It is the mechanism that gets emotions under control *before* the market opens — so you're not making decisions reactively while the market is running.

### Step 1: Background — What Is Driving This Market?

Before you look at a single chart, ask: **Is this market trading on its own, or is it being driven by something external?**

Markets driven by correlations to other markets (S&P inverse to dollar, bonds moving on European sovereign debt news) behave differently and are harder to trade with pure profile analysis. When the S&P is tick-for-tick inverse to the DXY dollar index, profile signals in the S&P alone will mislead you.

> *"Those are markets with low conviction. Traders get cut up on very badly sometimes because they have no idea what's going on."*

Start your prep with a one-sentence background statement: *"The S&P is currently driven by dollar movements / bond spreads / its own structure."* That anchors everything else.

### Step 2: Top-Down Analysis (Monthly → Weekly → Daily)

Always start from the highest timeframe and work down. The longer the timeframe, the more authority it has.

> *"Which takes precedence — the weekly or the daily? It's going to be the weekly, because the longer the timeframe, the more relevant it is."*

**Monthly Chart:**
- What is the long-term structural context?
- Where are the major reference levels?
- Is the market in a balance area, or trending?
- Where is excess on the monthly timeframe?

**Weekly Chart:**
- Where are we within the longer-term structure?
- Is the weekly trend aligned with the monthly?
- Are there weekly reference levels the daily is approaching?

**Daily Chart:**
- What is today's balance or trend context?
- Where are the reference points for today's session?
- What is the value area trend (higher, lower, unchanged, overlapping)?

Only after working top-down do you develop your intraday strategy.

### Step 3: What Timeframe Is Controlling?

After the top-down review, ask: who is currently in control of this market?

- **Day timeframe control**: market barely moves outside yesterday's range; very mechanical
- **Short-term control**: market moves assertively out of yesterday's range; 3–5 day trend
- **Intermediate-term control**: market moving purposefully over multiple weeks; hedge fund-style moves
- **Long-term control**: major structural move; Fidelity/Vanguard-type participation

Identifying the controlling timeframe determines your strategy (scalp vs. hold) and your risk (tighter vs. wider stops).

### Step 4: Inside Days and Trading Ranges

An **inside day** (where today's range is completely within yesterday's range) is a form of balance:

> *"An inside day is a form of balance. I find very little incentive to take any trade home from balance. In balance, the market is looking for more information before it makes its next directional move."*

Your three scenarios: stays in balance, looks outside and fails, breaks out and goes.

For trading ranges in general: identify the extremes, mark the excess at each end, and know that the center of the range is the reference for trade location.

### Step 5: Trends Within Trends

> *"Trends are not mutually exclusive. You can have a long-term trend up. You can have an intermediate-term trend down within the long-term trend up. Very natural."*

When someone asks "is the market trending?" — the answer is always "it depends on your timeframe." The S&P can be in a long-term uptrend, a 3-week intermediate downtrend, and a 2-day short-term uptend — simultaneously. Know which trend applies to your trade.

### Step 6: News Calendar

Know what is scheduled **before** the session begins. You don't need to predict the number — you need to know **when** the announcement is coming.

> *"I do not want to know what the news announcement is. I want to know when it's coming. I want to read the market's reaction to it. I want to see how the market reacts in there."*

The market's reaction to news is the signal, not the news itself. A strong structure that barely dips on bad news and then rallies is telling you something far more reliable than the headline.

## Lesson 5: The Narrative — The Most Skipped Step

### What a Narrative Is

After all your preparation — background, top-down, references, timeframe — you write a short narrative. In plain language. On paper (or a document).

> *"The narrative is nothing more than putting into words what you're seeing in the marketplace. It's the recap of all the work you've done."*

Example narrative: *"I'm in a 10-day balance. My trade location is at the top of that balance. Structure has not been good on the upside. Volume has been declining. I'm looking for a potential break to the downside, and my destination trade would be the low end of the 10-day range."*

That's it. Concise. Direct. Actionable.

### Why Almost Nobody Does It

> *"The number of people that actually will do the narrative is almost minuscule. Sounds good, but everybody knows they understand it — they know what's going on. They don't need to do that."*

Then Dalton delivers the diagnostic:

> *"When you go to write a narrative, all of a sudden the words don't flow very easily. What do you think your grasp is of what's really going on? It's not very good."*

If you can't write it, you don't actually understand it. The act of writing forces you to be explicit about your analysis — and gaps in your understanding become immediately obvious.

Conversely, if you truly understand the market context, the narrative comes out smooth and fast. That fluency is the goal.

### The Narrative vs. Marking References

Many traders mistake **marking references on a chart** for preparation. Drawing lines, adding notations, color-coding levels — that feels productive. It is. But it's the linear step, not the synthesis step.

> *"That's linear work of marking the references. Easy work. Good work. Boy, you really feel busy. But you haven't gone to the other step of writing the narrative."*

References are analysis. The narrative is synthesis. You need both.

### Trading Scenarios — The Last Part of the Narrative

The final section of your narrative should contain **2–3 specific trading scenarios** for the upcoming session.

> *"You ought to have two or three things that you know you want to do if you get the right opportunity tomorrow. It's a lot easier to react when you already have thought about it."*

Scenario example:
- *If we open within yesterday's value and drive lower in the first period → I will look short targeting the bracket low*
- *If we open outside yesterday's value to the upside → I will look for a test and rejection back into value as a short*
- *If we open and immediately drive higher through last week's high → I will get long immediately and monitor for continuation*

When the market opens and delivers Scenario 2 — you act. You don't freeze. You don't deliberate. You've already thought this through.

## Lesson 6: Full Week Walkthrough — April 9–16

This is where it all comes together. Dalton walks through a full week in the S&P applying every concept from all four DVDs.

### Friday, April 9

The market opened, attempted down, formed a **poor low** early (no tail). Sellers never showed up. Momentum traders pushed prices back up late in the day — but with **no elongation**.

Key observations:
- Poor low = unfinished business on the downside
- No elongation = long-term sellers had limited interest in pushing higher
- A late spike formed to the upside — a probe that may or may not be accepted

Assessment going into the weekend: *"I thought this was momentum traders playing a game on Friday afternoon. I didn't think it could hold this — no volume, no shape, no conviction."*

### Monday, April 12

- The market opened and **stayed within the spike** (Friday's spike accepted — positive)
- Value was **higher** — bullish confirmation
- But: **no elongation, wide POC** — long-term buyers not present
- **Poor low** again from the overnight
- Sellers remain absent — positive
- Two poor lows now stacking: *"One poor low might be minus 5. Two poor lows back to back — maybe minus 25, minus 30. You get exponential expansion of evidence."*

Conflicting information: value higher (good) but two poor lows building (bad). This is normal.

> *"If there was no conflicting information, the trend would be going in one direction and everybody would know it. Opportunity would be very limited."*

### Tuesday, April 13

- Structure repair attempted
- Buying tail formed — positive
- But poor low still not corrected — it carries forward

### Wednesday, April 14

- **Anomalies** visible (jagged edge in structure)
- Market can't close a prior gap — this is a buy signal when positioned correctly
- Overnight low exactly at the anomaly level AND the half back — multiple references converging
- *"Perfect day timeframe buy"*

### Thursday, April 15

- Market opens **at the spike base** from earlier in the week
- Market builds higher but can't elongate — still running on day timeframe only
- POC only 2 ticks off center
- **Almost perfectly balanced day**
- Another poor low forming

Assessment Thursday night: *"No real evidence of long-term buyers. Quiet selling. Running on fumes. Another poor low. The odds against the longs are building."*

### Friday, April 16

The week culminates:

- Market opens **out of balance** — meaningful right away
- **'b' formation** developing (buying tail at the low, then selling pressure takes over upper half — the pattern of short covering without follow-through)
- Structure has been weakening all week
- Three accumulated poor lows
- Dalton's note: *"The poor underlying structure coming into Friday had increased the odds against the longs."*

Then the break comes.

> *"Usually on a big break, three structural references will contain the break. The market's structure played a role in setting up the move AND in containing it."*

The critical nuance of the entire week:

> *"It did not predict the break. Very often we see a day timeframe struggling to trade higher, structure weakens, volume declines — however, price doesn't always decline. But the ODDS have changed."*

This is the whole course in one sentence. The framework doesn't predict. It computes odds. And when enough evidence accumulates — poor highs, poor lows, no elongation, laggard behavior — the odds against the prevailing direction build to the point where the smart money is already positioned the other way.

## Lesson 7: Odds-Based Thinking — The Final Mental Shift

### You Can't Control Return. You Can Control Risk.

> *"We can control risk. We cannot control return."*

Every trade you make should be a risk management decision first. Where am I wrong? How much do I risk? What structural reference tells me I'm wrong?

Trade location — entering near a meaningful reference — is the primary tool for controlling risk. If you buy near a clear excess low, you know exactly where you're wrong: the excess breaks. If you buy in the middle of a range with no reference nearby, your stop is arbitrary and your risk is undefined.

### We Are Not Naturally Odds-Based Thinkers

> *"We are just not naturally programmed to be odds-based thinkers. We take exceptional risks that we never think about. We drive cars on ice and believe four-wheel drive works on all surfaces."*

Trading requires explicitly overriding this tendency. You must train yourself to think: *What are the odds? What evidence supports this trade? What evidence argues against it?*

The diffusion model, excess, balance, references, day type, opening type, volume — all of these are inputs to the odds calculation. The more factors that align, the higher the odds. Never certainty. Always odds.

### Emotional Capital Is More Valuable Than Financial Capital

> *"Not only do you lose financial capital — more importantly, when that happens, you lose something even more valuable: emotional capital. When you lose emotional capital, you lose confidence. When you lose confidence, it is very difficult to trade and trade smartly."*

This is why position sizing and trade location matter so much. A bad trade that costs you $500 financially might cost you $5,000 in emotional capital — the paralysis, second-guessing, and fear that follow. Protect both kinds of capital.

## Lesson 8: The Field of Vision — Integration

### What "Having the Field of Vision" Looks Like

You see it all simultaneously:
- Where we are in the long-term trend (monthly)
- What the intermediate structure looks like (weekly)
- What yesterday told you (daily structure, value area, tails, POC)
- Who is dominating today (opening type, timeframe behavior)
- What the auction is trying to do (attempted direction, volume)
- Where the next reference is (destination)
- What the odds are (evidence for and against)

You're not staring at price. You're seeing the whole field.

> *"When I'm trading well, it's like hitting a golf ball really well. You change your grip — it's nothing but hook and slice and pain. And then one time you hit it and it just feels perfect. And when I'm trading like that — that's the feeling. And it makes it all worth it."* — Julia (student)

### It Becomes Intuitive

> *"With more experience, the more intuitive it comes. The first time I was on a Segway — it's not intuitive. But within 10 minutes, all of a sudden you realize how intuitive it is. You're navigating around fire hydrants without even thinking. That's kind of what happens to you over a period of time."*

The goal isn't to consciously run through a checklist every trade. The goal is to internalize the framework so deeply that your mind automatically integrates all the inputs. That takes time. That's the year Dalton mentions at the beginning of DVD 1.
